Three speech recognizers
on meeting speech

Bashir Sadeghi · 25 November 2026 · Colab reproduction verified

On 500 shared clips, word error rate (WER) was 9.87% for FUTO, 13.67% for Whisper Turbo, and 14.12% for Whisper large-v3. WER divides word insertions, deletions, and substitutions by reference words. Lower is better.

01Recreate the leaderboard

I chose the Hugging Face Open ASR Leaderboard v1.16.2 because it provides public evaluation data and scoring code. I reproduce its AMI-Cleaned meeting-speech task, drawn from the Augmented Multiparty Interaction (AMI) corpus.

The two Whisper systems compare related automatic speech recognition (ASR) models. FUTO adds a connectionist temporal classification (CTC) recognizer with text correction. This compares shipped systems; it does not isolate architecture alone.

Subset: 7,715 of 7,805 test clips remain after excluding empty normalized references and scoring-ignore segments. I crossed 16 meeting sessions with three global duration groups containing approximately equal numbers of clips. From these 48 groups, I selected 500 clips approximately in proportion to group size, with at least two per group. Seed 17 and pinned data fix the selection; no model errors enter sampling. The shared sample contains 31.4 audio minutes and 5,473 normalized reference words.

Why 500: the corpus estimate scales each group’s sampled error and reference-word totals by its eligible clip count divided by its sampled clip count. A stratified bootstrap resamples saved clip counts 2,000 times with a finite-population correction; no models rerun. The observed interval widths of 2.2–3.1 percentage points support an exploratory comparison. FUTO’s interval lies below both Whisper intervals. The Whisper estimates are close; a paired interval for their difference was not computed, so this analysis does not establish a reliable Whisper winner.

Limits and differences: intervals cover sampling this fixed eligible corpus, not new meetings, reference errors, or environment changes. This was an exploratory precision check, not a prespecified stopping rule. The sample can grow while retaining earlier predictions if greater precision is needed.

All three runs used an NVIDIA T4 graphics processing unit (GPU) in Colab, batch size 4, and pinned model/data revisions. Whisper used English greedy decoding. FUTO used tier (256, 32), while the reference runner specifies (256, 64), a different revision, and optimized execution. Sampling and runner differences may explain score differences; their effects were not isolated.

02Test normalization

I applied the same rules to references and predictions, then re-scored saved transcripts. Level 0 uses the leaderboard rules. Levels 1–8 successively count differences in hesitations, name spellings, spelling variants, number wording, contractions, word spacing, case, and punctuation. Each level retains earlier restrictions. All levels still clean annotations, Unicode, accents, and whitespace.

At level 8, WER reaches 29.19% for Turbo, 29.41% for large-v3, and 20.32% for FUTO. Counting hesitations alone adds 2.88, 2.80, and 0.08 percentage points respectively. Punctuation gives the largest step; FUTO remains lowest at all nine levels. WER need not increase at every step because token alignment and reference-word counts can change.

Decision: use level 0 for leaderboard comparison, and stricter rules when a product needs faithful wording or formatting.

03Look beyond WER

WER weights all word edits equally. I added character error rate (CER), which divides character edits by reference characters, including spaces; and semantic distance, calculated as 1 minus cosine similarity between pinned MiniLM text embeddings. Semantic distance uses lightly cleaned original text and averages clips.

Useful disagreement: for “Which is the highest, exactly,” Turbo predicts “Which is nice, exactly,” while large-v3 predicts “Which is the highest, isn’t it?” WER favors Turbo (40% versus 60%); semantic distance favors large-v3 (0.052 versus 0.700), which preserves “highest.”

Failure case: for a reference targeting “sixty to eighty” year-old customers, FUTO produces “sixty two to eighty.” Semantic distance nevertheless favors FUTO (0.018) over Whisper’s “60 to 80” (0.066). WER and CER tie. This model-based metric misses an important number change.

A product team choosing ASR for meeting notes can use metric disagreements to prioritize human review. No metric here verifies factual accuracy. These examples were selected after scoring and interpreted against references; their audio was not independently verified.

04Characterize the data—and check it

All three systems have lower WER on longer clips. Low-density clips have the highest WER using either word-count rule. These are associations: density and WER both depend on reference-word counts, and subgroup uncertainty was not estimated.

Reliability: all 500 identifier-derived durations agree with decoded audio within one sample at 16 kHz; no duration groups change. All 16 session labels match the catalog, which validates labels, not audio identity. Normalization changes density groups for 76/500 clips (15.2%); the overall low-density finding remains.

Human review: I selected 3 shortest, 3 longest, 2 lowest-density, 2 highest-density, and 2 largest density-change clips, without predictions or WER. The review found 10 matching references and 2 omissions: “tried a few times” and “things like that.” I retained original references for leaderboard comparison. This targeted check cannot estimate the overall reference-error rate.

Decision: duration supports segmentation and workload analysis; density can flag clips for review, but depends on transcript quality and normalization.

Reproducibility, automation, and AI use

The README gives three prediction commands followed by one evaluation command. To re-score included Colab transcripts without rerunning ASR, run python main.py evaluate --submitted. This generates all metrics and reports in runs/recheck/; it may download the MiniLM encoder.

Automation: I would trigger one job on a model update or a schedule: run the fixed sample, check complete predictions, generate reports, then compare against the previous run and notify a reviewer when predefined tolerances are crossed. Prediction, completeness checks, and reporting work today. Scheduling, comparisons across runs, and notifications would be added. Listening and interpreting flagged changes remain human tasks.

AI contribution: OpenAI Codex helped find sources, implement the workflow, diagnose failures, and draft the report. It reproduced all 27 WER/CER score pairs and WER bootstrap intervals from saved transcripts. References came from the dataset; listening judgments came from a person.

My work: I chose the expandable 500-clip sample, proposed the normalization curve, reviewed the code and metric disagreements, and ran all three models and evaluation on Colab. I investigated FUTO’s 401 empty Mac GPU transcripts by comparing the same clip on the central processing unit (CPU) and GPU, reran it on the CPU, and arranged the human listening review.

Conclusion: FUTO is the strongest candidate for further AMI testing under these configurations. This study does not establish a reliable Whisper ranking, generalization to other domains, or production readiness.

Sources and evidence: leaderboard v1.16.2 · pinned AMI-Cleaned data · leaderboard scorer · SemDist paper. This project uses a MiniLM-based variant. Full configurations, interactive figures, transcripts, and checks are in the repository.
